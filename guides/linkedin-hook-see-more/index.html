How to Write a LinkedIn Hook Before “See More”

A LinkedIn post can be up to 3,000 characters long, but most readers decide whether to read it from the first two or three lines. Everything after that sits behind a “…see more” link. This guide explains how the fold works, why there is no single safe character count, and how to write an opening that makes expanding the post worth it.

Open the LinkedIn Character Counter to draft your post and check its length as you write. Your text stays in your browser.

What the “see more” fold actually is

When a post is longer than the space LinkedIn gives it in the feed, the text is truncated and a “see more” link is added. LinkedIn does not publish the exact cutoff. In practice the visible preview is a small number of lines, and the number of characters those lines hold depends on several things you do not control:

  • Device and window width. A wide desktop column shows more characters per line than a phone held upright.
  • Line breaks. A short first line followed by a break uses a whole line of the preview, even if it is only five words.
  • Character width. “Illuminating” and “WWWWWW” take very different amounts of space in a proportional font.
  • App version and layout changes. LinkedIn adjusts its feed design from time to time.

You will often see figures such as “about 200 characters on desktop” or “about 140 on mobile” quoted online. They are rough observations, not documented rules. A safer working assumption is that your first sentence, and possibly only part of your second, will be seen. Write so that those words carry the point on their own.

What a good hook does

A hook is not a trick. It is a promise the rest of the post keeps. A good opening does three jobs in very few words:

  1. Names the subject, so the right readers recognize it is for them.
  2. Signals value: a result, a lesson, a number, a surprise or a useful list.
  3. Leaves a reason to continue: the how, the why, the example or the steps.

Openings that only create curiosity (“You won’t believe what happened today…”) may get a click, but readers who expand and find little behind it learn to scroll past you next time. Clarity tends to age better than suspense.

Before and after: rewriting a weak opening

Before (a typical announcement):

“I’m thrilled and humbled to announce that after an incredible journey with so many amazing people, I’ve accepted a new role as…”

The first 120 characters contain no information a reader can use. Now compare:

After:

“I’m joining Northwind as Head of Support. Three things from my last role I’m taking with me:”

The news is in the first line, and the second line promises something useful to people who are not the author’s friends. The rest of the post can deliver those three things in short paragraphs.

A step-by-step method

  1. Write the post body first. It is easier to summarise a finished argument than to invent a hook for an unwritten one.
  2. Find the single most useful sentence in your draft: a number, a conclusion or a counter-intuitive finding. That is usually your opening.
  3. Cut it to one line. Aim for a first sentence under about 100 characters. Remove adverbs, hedges and setup phrases.
  4. Decide on the second line. Either complete the promise (“Here’s the checklist.”) or add the specific context that makes the first line credible.
  5. Check the length. Paste the whole post into the LinkedIn counter. Keep well under 3,000 characters; leave room for edits after pasting.
  6. Preview on a phone. If you can, look at the post on a narrow screen before publishing, or right after, and edit if the fold cuts the point in half.

Formatting choices that affect the fold

Line breaks make posts scannable, but a break after every short sentence pushes your second idea below the fold. Use a break after the hook, then group related sentences into short paragraphs. Emoji at the start of a line can work as visual bullets but should not replace words; screen readers announce each emoji’s name. Unicode “bold” letters made from mathematical alphanumeric symbols look striking but are read poorly by assistive technology and are not found by search the way normal text is. Use them rarely, if at all. Hashtags and mentions at the very start push your actual message further back; place them at the end unless the mention is the point.

The rest of the post still matters

A reader who clicks “see more” is giving you attention on credit. Repay it quickly: deliver the promised steps, example or explanation in the next few lines, keep each paragraph to one idea, and finish with a clear takeaway or a specific question. If the topic needs more than you can say comfortably in a post, publish a LinkedIn article or a page on your own site and use the post to summarise it.

Frequently asked questions

How many characters show before “see more” on LinkedIn?

LinkedIn does not publish a fixed number, and the visible amount varies by device, layout and line breaks. Plan for your first sentence to be visible and treat any specific number you read online as an estimate.

Do line breaks count toward LinkedIn’s 3,000-character limit?

Yes. Line breaks are part of the text. They also affect how much of your post fits above the fold, because each one starts a new visual line.

Should every post start with a hook?

Every post should start with its point. For a short post that fits entirely above the fold, there is nothing to hook into; just say the thing clearly.

Does a longer post perform better?

Length alone is not a reliable signal. A post should be as long as the useful content and no longer. The LinkedIn post character limit guide explains the documented 3,000-character rule.
