GOOGLE ADS COPYWRITING GUIDE · NOVEMBER 2026

How to Write Google Ads Responsive Search Ad Headlines

A responsive search ad (RSA) accepts up to 15 headlines of 30 characters and up to 4 descriptions of 90 characters. Google Ads then mixes them into many different ads. Writing for that system is less about one perfect line and more about a set of distinct, accurate messages that read well in any order. This guide gives a repeatable method, a worked example and the policy pitfalls that cause rejections.

Open the Google Ads Character Counter to draft all your headlines and descriptions with live limit checks. For the field limits in detail, see Google Ads character limits.

How Google assembles a responsive search ad

According to Google’s help page on responsive search ads, you must enter at least 3 headlines and 2 descriptions, and can enter up to 15 and 4. When the ad serves, Google selects at least one headline and one description, can show up to three headlines in the headline position, and tests combinations over time to learn which work best for different searches. Headline text can also appear at the start of a description, and unused headlines may be shown as link-style assets.

Two consequences follow. First, every headline must make sense on its own, because you do not know which others it will appear with. Second, variety matters: fifteen near-identical headlines give the system nothing to test, and Google’s repetition policy disallows asset text that repeats words or phrases within the same ad group.

Spaces and punctuation count. Google states that every character in a double-width language such as Chinese, Japanese or Korean counts as two, so a Japanese headline has about 15 characters of room.

A method: write by angle, not by variation

Instead of rephrasing one idea fifteen times, give each headline a job. A useful set of angles:

  1. Keyword match (2–3 headlines): the product or service in the words people search for. Google recommends including at least one of your keywords in your headlines.
  2. Primary benefit (2–3): what the customer gets, such as speed, savings or convenience.
  3. Proof (1–2): a verifiable fact such as years in business, a certification or a review count you can substantiate.
  4. Offer or price (1–2): a specific, current offer that matches the landing page.
  5. Objection handling (1–2): free returns, no contract, cancellation terms.
  6. Call to action (1–2): what to do next, such as “Book Online” or “Get a Quote”.
  7. Brand (1): your name, so it can appear even when other assets rotate.

Google’s own tips suggest writing the first three headlines as if they will appear together, then adding as many unique headlines as you can, for example by varying the call to action.

Worked example: a fictional accounting firm

Suppose a small firm, “Ledgerly”, offers bookkeeping for freelancers. A first draft of headlines, each checked against the 30-character limit:

Two descriptions can then do different jobs: one explains the service (“Monthly bookkeeping, VAT returns and year-end prep for freelancers and sole traders.”, 84 characters) and one reduces risk (“Fixed monthly price. Your own accountant. Switch from spreadsheets in a week.”, 77 characters). Every claim, including “First Month Free”, must be true on the landing page.

Pinning, and why to use it sparingly

Pinning fixes an asset to a position. Google notes that only assets pinned to Headline 1, Headline 2 or Description 1 are guaranteed to show; content pinned to Headline 3 or Description 2 is not shown in every ad. Pinning also reduces the combinations Google can test and can lower Ad Strength. Pin when you must, such as a legally required disclaimer or a regulated product name, and consider pinning two or three alternatives to the same slot so some testing still happens.

Ad Strength is a guide, not a goal

Ad Strength rates the relevance, quantity and diversity of your assets from “Poor” to “Excellent”. Google recommends at least two RSAs per ad group with “Good” or “Excellent” Ad Strength. It is a useful prompt to add more distinct headlines, but it does not measure whether your claims are compelling or accurate. Compare actual performance over time rather than writing to the meter.

Policy pitfalls that cause disapprovals

Google Ads’ editorial policy covers several issues that are easy to trip over when squeezing copy into 30 characters:

  • Capitalization used for emphasis rather than its intended purpose, such as “FREE” or “BEST PRICE”.
  • Punctuation and symbols used gimmickily, such as repeated exclamation marks or symbols replacing words.
  • Unacceptable spacing: missing spaces to save characters (“FastDelivery”) or extra spaces for effect.
  • Repetition of words or phrases within an asset or across assets in the same ad group.
  • Phone numbers in ad text; use a call asset instead.
  • Style and spelling: non-standard spelling or text that does not make sense.

Policies change; check the current wording in Google Ads before launching.

Dynamic text: keyword insertion

Keyword insertion, written as {KeyWord:Default text}, replaces the code with the keyword that triggered the ad when it fits, and with the default text when it does not. The default text must fit the 30-character limit on its own, and the inserted keyword must read naturally in the sentence. Insertion can produce awkward headlines with broad keyword lists, so preview the likely results.

A final checklist

  1. At least 8–10 distinct headlines, each under 30 characters, covering several angles.
  2. No headline depends on another to make sense.
  3. Descriptions do different jobs and each stays within 90 characters.
  4. Prices, offers and claims match the landing page today.
  5. Capitalization, punctuation and spacing follow normal style.
  6. Pins only where required.
  7. Validated in the Google Ads interface, which is the final authority.

Frequently asked questions

How many headlines should I write?

The minimum is 3 and the maximum 15. Google’s guidance encourages providing as many unique headlines as you can; quality and distinctness matter more than hitting 15.

Can I use the same headline in several ads?

Google’s repetition policy disallows asset text that repeats words or phrases within the same asset or another asset in the same ad group, campaign or account. Vary the wording and the angle.

Are RSA limits the same as SEO title limits?

No. The 30- and 90-character limits apply to ads. Organic search titles and meta descriptions are truncated by pixel width; use the SERP snippet preview for those.
