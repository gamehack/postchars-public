How to Structure an X (Twitter) Thread People Finish Reading

A thread is a series of connected posts that lets you explain something too big for one standard 280-character post. Good threads are planned like a short article: a clear opening, one idea per post, and an ending that pays off. This guide covers structure, the character budget each post really has, where to put links, and a worked example.

Open the X Thread Splitter to turn a draft into numbered posts whose weighted length, including the numbering, stays within 280.

Thread or single post?

Before you start, decide whether the idea needs a thread at all. A thread is a good fit for a step-by-step process, a story with several beats, a list of resources with a sentence on each, or a summary of a longer piece of work. It is a poor fit for one opinion padded out to look substantial; readers notice when post 4 repeats post 2. X Premium subscribers can publish much longer single posts, but many readers still see the opening few lines first, so the structural advice below applies either way.

The anatomy of a thread

PartJobTypical length
Opening postState the topic and the payoff; say it is a thread if that helpsOne post, often well under 280

SummaryThe takeaway in a sentence or a short listOne post

Writing the opening post

The first post is the only one most people see in their timeline. It should stand alone: if someone reads nothing else, they should still learn what the thread is about and why it is worth their time. Compare:

  • Vague: “Some thoughts on pricing 🧵”
  • Specific: “We raised prices 30% last year and lost 4% of customers. Here’s how we decided, what we told users, and what we’d change: 🧵”

The specific version names the result, signals what follows and sets expectations for length. It is 124 weighted characters in total, including 2 for the thread emoji, leaving plenty of room.

One idea per post

Each post should make one point that a reader could quote on its own. That makes posts easy to skim, easy to reply to, and easy to share individually. If you find yourself writing “and also” halfway through a post, that is usually where a new post should begin. A useful test: read each post in isolation. If it only makes sense after the previous one, add the minimum context (“Step 3:”, “The second reason:”) rather than relying on the reader’s memory.

The real character budget per post

Every post has 280 weighted characters under X’s published counting rules, which this site applies with the open-source twitter-text library. Several things eat into that budget:

ElementWeighted cost
Numbering such as “ (3/8)”6 for single digits; 8 once both numbers reach two digits, as in “ (10/12)”
Any recognized URL23, regardless of its visible length
Most emoji2 each
Many Chinese, Japanese and Korean characters2 each
Ordinary Latin letters, digits, spaces1 each

A post with a link and a “(10/12)” suffix therefore has about 249 weighted characters left for words. The thread splitter accounts for the suffix automatically, and the X character counter shows the weighted total for any single post. For the details of URL and emoji weighting, see how X counts URLs, emoji and Chinese characters.

Numbering: when it helps

Numbering such as “1/8” tells readers how long the thread is and helps people reference a specific post. It costs characters and commits you to a count, so number only after the thread is final. For short threads of three or four posts, numbering is optional; for long ones it is a courtesy. If you add a post later, update every number, or use a format without a total such as “1/”.

Where to put links

A link in the opening post competes with the thread itself: readers who tap it leave before reading the rest. Many writers place the main link in the final post, after the reader has seen the value, or in a reply to the opening post. Whatever you choose, describe what the link leads to in words, because the preview card may not appear or may be cropped.

A worked example

Here is a five-post outline for a thread about preparing a conference talk:

  1. “I’ve given 40 conference talks. The prep process that finally stopped me running over time, in 5 steps: 🧵”
  2. “1. Write the one sentence you want people to remember. Every slide either supports it or gets cut.”
  3. “2. Draft the talk as an outline, not a script. Scripts make you read; outlines make you talk.”
  4. “3. Rehearse with a timer at 80% of your slot. Real talks run long because of pauses, laughs and clicker trouble.”
  5. “4. Cut the last 10% of slides before the final rehearsal, not after. Then share your slides: [link]”

Each post is a self-contained tip, each is well under 280 weighted characters, and the link arrives at the end. You would adjust the opening promise (“in 5 steps”) to match the final count.

Editing checklist before you post

  1. The first post works on its own and states the payoff.
  2. Each post makes one point and can be read in isolation.
  3. No post is over 280 weighted characters, including numbering and links.
  4. Numbers match the final count.
  5. Repetition has been cut: if two posts say the same thing, merge them.
  6. The final post summarises, links or asks a specific question.
  7. You have read the whole thread once more as a reader, top to bottom.

Frequently asked questions

How long should an X thread be?

As long as it takes to make each point once. Many useful threads are five to ten posts. If a thread passes about fifteen posts, consider whether it would work better as an article with a short thread pointing to it.

Does the thread emoji count toward the limit?

Yes. 🧵 is an emoji and counts as two weighted characters under twitter-text rules.

Can PostChars post the thread for me?

No. The thread splitter is a local drafting tool. Copy each post into X to publish. Always confirm the final count in X, which is the authority on its own limits.
