What is keyword density?

Keyword density estimates how often a selected word or exact phrase appears relative to the total word count. This tool uses occurrences ÷ total words × 100, displays the 20 most frequent terms, and distinguishes one-, two- and three-word phrases. Other tools may use a different denominator for multi-word phrases, so percentages are not always directly comparable.

Why should you check repeated phrases?

Frequency analysis helps editors spot accidental repetition, inconsistent wording and overused product phrases. For a landing page, a repeated phrase might be intentional, but a page that reads like a list of keywords usually serves readers poorly. Frequency alone does not measure relevance, search intent, originality or quality.

What does “Hide all-stopword phrases” mean?

Common English function words such as “the” and “and” can dominate the table. Hiding phrases made entirely of common words makes the table easier to scan while preserving the original total word count. For longer phrases, sequences containing useful words remain eligible.

How to check keyword frequency without over-optimizing

  1. Paste the actual draft you intend to publish.
  2. Review single words for repetitive vocabulary or terminology drift.
  3. Switch to two- and three-word phrases to find repeated expressions.
  4. Edit for reader clarity; do not chase a fixed density percentage.

Frequently asked questions

Does a higher keyword density improve ranking?

There is no universal percentage that guarantees ranking. The target phrase comparison is a writing diagnostic, not an SEO pass/fail score. A phrase-frequency table can help editing but does not substitute for answering the searcher's question.

Does this tool upload my content?

No. The text and calculations stay in this browser tab and are not sent to PostChars.

Can I count phrases rather than individual words?

Yes. Select two-word or three-word phrases to inspect repeated sequences of tokens.

A worked example

Suppose a 400-word product page mentions “standing desk” 12 times. The target phrase density is 12 ÷ 400 × 100 = 3%. That number on its own says little. What matters is whether the repetitions read naturally. If eight of them appear in the first paragraph and the page never mentions height range, motor noise or weight capacity, the problem is not the percentage but the missing information. Replacing some repetitions with specific details usually improves both readability and usefulness.

How words are counted here

Text is lowercased and split into words made of letters and digits, with internal apostrophes and hyphens kept, so “standing-desk” is one token and “desk's” is one. Phrase mode counts every run of two or three consecutive words. With the stop-word filter on, phrases made only of common words such as “of the” or “in a” are hidden. The target phrase must match consecutive words exactly, after lowercasing.

Search engines do not publish an ideal keyword density, and Google's guidance on spam policies warns against keyword stuffing.
